The Observability Department of Everything

October 30, 2026

How observability teams inherit everyone else's reliability problems, and why owning the tools shouldn't mean owning the outcomes.

There’s a peculiar thing that happens in engineering organizations where a team gradually becomes responsible for things nobody actually agreed they should own. No meeting, no RFC, no grand announcement. Just a series of perfectly reasonable requests that, taken together, become something profoundly unreasonable.

Observability seems particularly susceptible to this.

Someone needs a dashboard. Observability. Alerts aren’t firing? Observability. SLOs? Obviously Observability. A service is slow and nobody knows why? Well, the logs are in your system. Congratulations. You now own the problem.

How Did We Get Here?

I’ve spent a good portion of my career in Site Reliability Engineering, and more recently in Observability. I understand how we got here. Historically, observability tooling lived with SRE and infrastructure teams. Those engineers needed to understand what production systems were doing, and somebody had to keep Prometheus running and Grafana from consuming all available memory.

A Word About System Integrity Protection

If you’re running macOS 26 or later, this approach requires disabling System Integrity Protection (SIP). I want to be clear about this upfront: disabling SIP is a bad security practice. It removes protections that Apple built into macOS to prevent unauthorized system modifications.

To disable SIP:

  1. Reboot into Recovery Mode (hold Command-R during startup)
  2. Open Terminal from the Utilities menu
  3. Run csrutil disable
  4. Reboot again

There are alternatives worth considering. Finder’s “Connect to Server” (Command-K) works fine for most use cases, mounting shares under /Volumes. Third-party applications like Mountain Duck or ExpanDrive handle network mounts without requiring SIP to be disabled. But if you need that clean /external path at the root filesystem level, this is what it takes.

How I sound shouldn’t be a distraction if I’m talking with my colleagues on Zoom. I use a variety of microphones during the day. My team can tell you I call from coffee shops a number of times, which can be noisy depending on who ordered an espresso. My home office/bedroom doesn’t have much on the walls or floor, making it perfect for echoes. So while one microphone maybe good for one space, another one might be dreadful.
