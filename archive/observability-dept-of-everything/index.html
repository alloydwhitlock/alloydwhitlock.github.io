The Observability Department of Everything

October 30, 2026

How observability teams inherit everyone else's reliability problems, and why owning the tools shouldn't mean owning the outcomes.

There’s a peculiar thing that happens in engineering organizations where a team gradually becomes responsible for things nobody actually agreed they should own. No meeting, no RFC, no grand announcement. Just a series of perfectly reasonable requests that, taken together, become something profoundly unreasonable.

Observability seems particularly susceptible to this.

Someone needs a dashboard. Observability. Alerts aren’t firing? Observability. SLOs? Obviously Observability. A service is slow and nobody knows why? Well, the logs are in your system. Congratulations. You now own the problem.

How Did We Get Here?

I’ve spent a good portion of my career in Site Reliability Engineering, and more recently in Observability. Historically, observability tooling lived with SRE and infrastructure teams. Those engineers needed to understand what production systems were doing, and somebody had to keep Prometheus running and Grafana from consuming all available memory.

But somewhere along the way, owning the tools started looking suspiciously like owning the outcomes. Apparently, if you can display a problem on a dashboard, you’re responsible for solving it. By that logic, the weather app owes me an apology every time it rains.

I think some of this comes from how we’ve come to think about SRE. Incident response, SLOs, and monitoring are all important parts of operating reliable systems, but they’re not the entirety of the discipline. The engineering work that originally drew me to SRE was getting inside production systems, understanding how they behaved, and making substantial changes to improve them.

SLOs are a good example of where the distinction matters. They’re useful tools for deciding where to invest engineering effort. They help teams agree on what reliability means for their users. But the people building and operating a service need to be part of those decisions, and ultimately responsible for improving the system. Observability can provide the measurements without becoming the owner of what they measure.

Observability Is Engineering, Too

Observability has inherited a good deal of this work because we own the machinery used to measure it. Once you’re responsible for collecting metrics, it’s apparently a small leap to deciding which metrics matter, setting thresholds, and determining who should be paged. Each individual request is reasonable. The collection of them can become something else entirely.

Meanwhile, there’s an actual engineering discipline here. Moving billions of events through ingestion pipelines, dealing with backpressure, cardinality, storage, query performance, and the increasingly absurd cost of keeping all this data around is substantial work. These are products we’re building, with their own customers, architectural problems, and reliability requirements.

There’s a certain irony in expecting the observability team to help everyone improve their systems while giving them less time to improve the tools everyone depends on. I’m sure there’s a dashboard somewhere that can tell us how that’s going.

Helping Isn’t Owning

None of this means observability engineers should barricade themselves behind a wall of dashboards and tell everyone to read the documentation. Quite the opposite. We should work alongside other engineers, improve instrumentation, understand their problems, and sometimes get into application code to help solve them.

Good observability should make engineers more capable of understanding their own systems, not more dependent on another team to do it for them. That’s a product problem worth solving, and one I think we should take seriously.

If your service is slow, we should make it easy to understand why. If the metrics are missing, the logs are useless, or the query takes longer than the incident, that’s our problem to fix. But understanding the behavior of your service, deciding what matters, and driving improvements should remain close to the people building it.

That’s not an argument for throwing problems over a wall. It’s an argument for making sure we’re solving them together without accidentally moving ownership.

The Problem With Being Helpful

I don’t think anyone deliberately designed this arrangement. It’s what happens when responsibility follows convenience rather than ownership. A few favors become expectations, expectations become process, and eventually nobody remembers things could work any other way. The more helpful the observability team becomes, the more likely it is to inherit the next vaguely related responsibility.

I’d rather we didn’t let that happen. Observability has plenty of interesting engineering problems to solve, and a pretty important job helping everyone else solve theirs.

We should be building systems that make engineers better at understanding and improving their software, not quietly relieving them of that responsibility.

Otherwise, we’ll end up with a team that’s exceptionally good at telling everyone their systems are broken, and too busy doing that to fix anything of its own.
